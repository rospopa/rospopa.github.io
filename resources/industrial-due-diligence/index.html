Industrial property due diligence: physical and environmental

This guide organizes the physical, environmental, title, survey, zoning, lease, and utility questions that commonly affect industrial property acquisitions in the Chicago-area counties served by ROSPOPA.

Published October 3, 2026 · Educational, not legal, environmental, engineering, or investment advice.

In brief

  • Industrial diligence is different because prior uses, heavy systems, loading, utilities, and environmental history can drive value and closing risk.
  • The purchase agreement should control deadlines, access, seller deliverables, and remedies for findings.
  • Environmental review often starts with a Phase I ESA and may expand to sampling, vapor, tanks, asbestos, lead, or regulatory closure issues.
  • Physical diligence should convert roof, slab, dock, utility, fire-protection, pavement, and drainage findings into a capital plan.
  • Findings usually become a decision about price, credits, escrows, repairs, insurance, indemnities, or whether to proceed.

Why industrial diligence is different

Industrial property diligence is not limited to rent, taxes, and a walk-through. Past and current operations may involve chemicals, fuels, tanks, floor drains, heavy equipment, vehicle repair, food handling, manufacturing residues, or unusual utility loads.

Physical systems also carry deal risk. A roof, slab, dock package, sprinkler system, transformer, truck court, or drainage problem can change occupancy timing and capital needs.

For a broader acquisition framework, see the investment diligence guide. For building-fit questions, use the industrial evaluation checklist and the building types and specifications guide.

Organizing the diligence period

The purchase and sale agreement should state when the diligence period starts, what the seller must deliver, what access is allowed, and what happens if the buyer objects. Calendar every notice date before ordering reports.

Access needs should be specific. Environmental consultants, roofers, surveyors, engineers, lenders, insurers, contractors, appraisers, and utility representatives may need different site visits, tenant coordination, and sampling permissions.

Sellers can reduce friction by pre-assembling the documents listed in the selling guide. A clean file does not replace diligence, but it helps buyers and lenders ask better questions.

  • Use a diligence tracker with document status, responsible party, deadline, and open issue.
  • Require insurance, safety rules, and escort procedures for invasive or operationally sensitive inspections.
  • Coordinate tenant notices and quiet-hours if the property is occupied.
  • Reserve enough time for follow-up testing when the initial report identifies a concern.

Environmental diligence

Environmental work is usually scoped by the property history, intended use, lender requirements, and the buyer's risk tolerance. The consultant and legal counsel should explain the standard, reliance language, and any data gaps.

Phase I ESA and AAI

A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment is commonly prepared under ASTM E1527-21 and federal All Appropriate Inquiries concepts. The U.S. EPA All Appropriate Inquiries page explains the federal framework and links to current rules.

The scope commonly includes records review, historical sources, site reconnaissance, interviews, and a report identifying recognized environmental conditions. The user also has responsibilities, such as providing specialized knowledge, commonly known information, and information about purchase price or environmental liens when applicable.

AAI has timing rules: the inquiry must be conducted or updated within one year before acquisition, and certain components must be conducted or updated within 180 days. Confirm current requirements and reliance language with the environmental consultant and counsel.

RECs, HRECs, and CRECs

A recognized environmental condition, or REC, points to a likely presence or release of hazardous substances or petroleum products. A historical recognized environmental condition, or HREC, generally indicates a past release that has been addressed to the applicable standard.

A controlled recognized environmental condition, or CREC, generally means a past release remains subject to controls, restrictions, or ongoing obligations. The words matter because they can affect lender review, buyer obligations, and future use.

Phase II sampling and vapor intrusion

A Phase II investigation may include soil, groundwater, soil-gas, indoor air, or other sampling. The purpose is to test specific concerns, not to prove that no risk exists anywhere on the property.

Vapor intrusion review can be important where volatile chemicals or petroleum releases may migrate into buildings. Sampling methods, seasonal conditions, building pressure, and mitigation design should be handled by qualified environmental professionals.

Asbestos, lead, PCBs, USTs, and other regulated materials

Older industrial buildings may require separate review for asbestos-containing materials, lead-based paint, PCBs in equipment or caulk, underground storage tanks, aboveground tanks, hydraulic lifts, sumps, and floor drains. These are often outside a basic Phase I scope unless specifically added.

Ask whether the consultant recommends targeted surveys before demolition, renovation, financing, or occupancy. Removal and disposal rules can affect timing and cost.

Illinois EPA SRP and NFR letters

The Illinois EPA Site Remediation Program is a voluntary cleanup program that can lead to a No Further Remediation letter for addressed conditions. An NFR letter is not the same as saying every environmental risk is gone.

Review the recorded terms, institutional controls, engineered barriers, exposure routes, and future-use limits. Counsel and the consultant should confirm whether the proposed use remains consistent with the closure.

Landowner liability protections

Federal landowner liability protections are concept-based and fact-specific. They may depend on proper AAI, continuing obligations, no affiliation issues, and conduct after acquisition.

Do not treat a report alone as a guarantee. Review the facts with environmental counsel before relying on any protection.

Physical diligence

Physical diligence should identify current condition, code or safety concerns, near-term repairs, long-term capital needs, and operational constraints. A property condition assessment may reference ASTM E2018 concepts through ASTM, but the scope should match the asset and the deal.

Roof, structure, and slab

Review roof age, membrane type, warranty, leaks, drainage, penetrations, insulation, repairs, and replacement timing. For structure and slab, inspect settlement, cracking, joints, floor flatness, load capacity, mezzanines, roof framing, and signs of impact or water intrusion.

Docks, doors, and equipment

Dock levelers, bumpers, seals, shelters, overhead doors, drive-in doors, controls, and restraints affect safety and operations. Field-test condition where practical and compare the loading package to the intended use.

Mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and fire protection

Mechanical, electrical, and plumbing review should cover remaining useful life, capacity, code concerns, maintenance records, and tenant-specific modifications. Electrical capacity should be confirmed against panels, service, transformer, utility data, and equipment requirements.

For fire protection, ask for sprinkler inspection reports, alarm records, deficiencies, hydraulic calculations, fire-pump tests if applicable, and any storage or commodity assumptions. Verify sprinkler design with a fire-protection engineer, insurer, and authority having jurisdiction.

Pavement, drainage, and flood

Pavement matters for trailers, forklifts, snow removal, drainage, and yard use. Inspect asphalt, concrete, catch basins, detention, ponding, striping, curbs, and truck-wear areas.

Flood and drainage review should include local records and the FEMA Flood Map Service Center. FEMA mapping does not replace a civil engineer's review of site drainage or stormwater obligations.

Accessibility, elevators, and cranes

Accessibility review should consider parking, routes, entrances, restrooms, office areas, signage, and any alterations. Elevators, hoists, cranes, bridge cranes, and jib cranes require records, load ratings, inspections, service contracts, and ownership clarification.

Deferred maintenance and capital plan

Convert inspection notes into a capital plan with timing, cost ranges, responsible party, and business impact. Separate immediate life-safety items from monitor-and-maintain items and future improvements.

Title, survey, and land

Title and survey review connects legal rights to physical use. The title commitment lists requirements and exceptions; the ALTA survey helps show boundaries, easements, improvements, access, and encroachments.

Review access rights, shared drive agreements, cross-parking, utilities, drainage easements, signage rights, recorded covenants, and any reciprocal easement agreements. A truck entrance that crosses another parcel needs documented rights, not just historical use.

For rail-served property, confirm rail agreements, switch rights, track maintenance, active service, clearance, and whether the railroad or a third party controls access. Rail value can disappear if the legal and physical service rights do not match the marketing description.

Zoning and permits

Zoning diligence should confirm the current district, permitted uses, special uses, variances, nonconforming rights, outdoor storage, parking, truck routing, screening, signage, and hours of operation. A zoning verification letter can help document how the municipality views the property.

Permits and certificates of occupancy show whether past work and uses were approved. Check for open permits, violations, expired approvals, fire-code issues, unpermitted mezzanines, and tenant improvements that may need closeout.

For local research starting points, use the nine-county hub and municipal records. For Chicago-specific research, see the Chicago commercial real estate guide.

Operational and lease diligence for leased industrial

Lease diligence should test whether the income, obligations, and operational rights shown in the rent roll are supported by signed documents. Request every lease, amendment, guaranty, side letter, estoppel, SNDA, and notice relevant to occupancy.

  • Estoppels: confirm rent, term, deposits, defaults, options, exclusive rights, and tenant claims.
  • SNDAs: coordinate lender, buyer, and tenant rights after a foreclosure or ownership change.
  • CAM reconciliations: test recoveries, caps, exclusions, reserves, management fees, and year-end balances.
  • Repair obligations: identify who pays for roof, structure, HVAC, pavement, dock equipment, fire protection, and capital replacements.
  • Hazardous materials clauses: compare lease rights to actual operations, permits, storage, and environmental reports.
  • Parking and trailer rights: confirm allocated spaces, yard rights, storage limits, and rules for containers or fleet vehicles.

The commercial leasing guide explains why lease labels do not replace a clause-by-clause review.

Utilities and infrastructure

Industrial users often need utility confirmation before they can commit. Electricity, gas, water, sanitary sewer, storm sewer, compressed air, fiber, and telecom should be reviewed against the user's equipment and occupancy schedule.

For much of the region, electrical capacity questions may involve ComEd, the serving utility, an electrical engineer, and the tenant's equipment vendor. Confirm service voltage, phase, amperage, transformer ownership, spare capacity, upgrade lead times, and shutdown requirements.

Gas, water, and sewer review should include meter size, pressure, discharge limits, floor drains, pretreatment, process water, fire flow, and municipal capacity. Fiber and telecom are separate from an address being generally serviceable.

Turning findings into price, credits, escrows, or repairs

Diligence findings are useful only when they lead to a clear decision. The buyer can accept the risk, renegotiate price, request a credit, create an escrow, require seller repairs, modify representations, obtain insurance, or terminate if the contract allows.

Sellers should distinguish between documented defects, buyer preferences, and speculative future upgrades. Buyers should distinguish between ordinary maintenance and issues that change value, financing, insurability, or lawful use.

Material findings should be tied to evidence: report page, photo, contractor proposal, municipal response, title exception, lease clause, or consultant memo. That keeps negotiations focused on facts.
