Industrial property due diligence: physical and environmental

Physical, environmental, title, survey, zoning, lease and utility diligence for industrial acquisitions across nine Illinois counties.

Educational, not legal, environmental, engineering, or investment advice.

In brief

  • Prior uses, heavy systems, loading, utilities, and environmental history can drive value and closing risk.
  • The purchase agreement should control deadlines, access, seller deliverables, and remedies for findings.
  • Environmental review often starts with a Phase I ESA and may expand to sampling, vapor, tanks, asbestos, lead, or closure issues.
  • Turn roof, slab, dock, utility, fire-protection, pavement, and drainage findings into a capital plan.
  • Findings inform price, credits, escrows, repairs, insurance, indemnities, or whether to proceed.
  • Owner lens: For an owner, diligence is also the start of the permanent building file. Preserve reports, permits, warranties, maintenance logs, environmental records, and repair decisions for lenders, insurers, tenants, and a future buyer.

Why industrial diligence is different

Beyond rent, taxes and a walk-through, investigate past and current chemicals, fuels, tanks, floor drains, heavy equipment, vehicle repair, food handling, manufacturing residues and unusual utility loads.

Roof, slab, docks, sprinklers, transformers, truck courts, and drainage can affect occupancy timing and capital needs.

Use the investment diligence guide for acquisitions, and the industrial evaluation checklist and building types and specifications guide for building fit.

Organizing the diligence period

The purchase agreement should set the diligence start, seller deliverables, access, and remedies for buyer objections. Calendar notice dates before ordering reports.

Specify access for environmental consultants, roofers, surveyors, engineers, lenders, insurers, contractors, appraisers, and utilities, including tenant coordination and sampling permissions.

Assemble the selling guide documents to ease review, not replace diligence.

  • Track document status, responsible party, deadline, and open issues.
  • Set insurance, safety, and escort rules for invasive or operationally sensitive inspections.
  • Coordinate tenant notices and quiet hours at occupied properties.
  • Allow time for follow-up testing if an initial report raises concerns.

Environmental diligence

Scope environmental work to history, intended use, lender requirements and buyer risk tolerance. Confirm standards, reliance language and data gaps with the consultant and counsel.

Phase I ESA and AAI

A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment is commonly prepared under ASTM E1527-21 and federal All Appropriate Inquiries concepts. The U.S. EPA All Appropriate Inquiries page explains the federal framework and links to current rules.

Scope commonly includes records, historical sources, site reconnaissance, interviews and a report of recognized environmental conditions. Users must supply specialized knowledge, commonly known information and applicable purchase-price and environmental-lien information.

AAI must be conducted or updated within one year before acquisition; certain components must be conducted or updated within 180 days. Confirm current requirements and reliance language with the consultant and counsel.

RECs, HRECs, and CRECs

A recognized environmental condition (REC) indicates likely presence or release of hazardous substances or petroleum. A historical recognized environmental condition (HREC) generally indicates a past release addressed to the applicable standard.

A controlled recognized environmental condition (CREC) generally means a past release remains subject to controls, restrictions, or obligations. These terms can affect lender review, buyer obligations, and future use.

Phase II sampling and vapor intrusion

A Phase II may sample soil, groundwater, soil gas, indoor air, or other media to test specific concerns. It cannot prove that no risk exists anywhere on the property.

Review vapor intrusion where volatile chemicals or petroleum releases may migrate into buildings. Qualified environmental professionals should address sampling methods, seasonal conditions, building pressure, and mitigation design.

Asbestos, lead, PCBs, USTs, and other regulated materials

Older industrial buildings may need separate surveys for asbestos, lead paint, PCBs in equipment or caulk, underground and aboveground tanks, hydraulic lifts, sumps, and floor drains. These are often outside a basic Phase I unless added.

Ask whether targeted surveys are needed before demolition, renovation, financing, or occupancy. Removal and disposal rules affect timing and cost.

Illinois EPA SRP and NFR letters

The voluntary Illinois EPA Site Remediation Program can yield a No Further Remediation letter for addressed conditions, not removal of every environmental risk.

Review the recorded terms, institutional controls, engineered barriers, exposure routes, and future-use limits. Counsel and the consultant should confirm whether the proposed use remains consistent with the closure.

Landowner liability protections

Federal landowner liability protections depend on specific facts, potentially including proper AAI, continuing obligations, no affiliation issues and post-acquisition conduct.

A report is no guarantee. Review facts with environmental counsel before relying on protection.

Physical diligence

Physical diligence covers condition, code and safety concerns, repairs, capital needs, and operational constraints. A property condition assessment may reference ASTM E2018; scope it to the asset and deal.

Roof, structure, and slab

Review roof age, membrane, warranty, leaks, drainage, penetrations, insulation, repairs, and replacement timing. Inspect structure and slab for settlement, cracks, joints, flatness, load capacity, mezzanines, roof framing, impact, and water intrusion.

Docks, doors, and equipment

Field-test dock levelers, bumpers, seals, shelters, overhead and drive-in doors, controls and restraints where practical. Assess safety, operations and fit for intended use.

Compare the fleet with gates, docks, truck courts, and overhead obstructions using the vehicle dimensions and turning-path reference. A qualified engineer should review swept paths and margins; cargo-box dimensions alone do not establish access.

Mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and fire protection

Review MEP useful life, capacity, code concerns, maintenance, and tenant modifications. Confirm electrical capacity against panels, service, transformer, utility data, and equipment.

For fire protection, request sprinkler inspections, alarm records, deficiencies, hydraulic calculations, applicable fire-pump tests, and storage or commodity assumptions. Verify design with a fire-protection engineer, insurer, and authority having jurisdiction.

Pavement, drainage, and flood

Inspect pavement, catch basins, detention, ponding, striping, curbs, and truck-wear areas for trailer, forklift, snow-removal, drainage, and yard needs.

Check local flood and drainage records and the FEMA Flood Map Service Center. FEMA maps do not replace a civil engineer's site-drainage or stormwater review.

Accessibility, elevators, and cranes

Review accessible parking, routes, entrances, restrooms, office areas, signage, and alterations. For elevators, hoists, bridge cranes, and jib cranes, confirm records, load ratings, inspections, service contracts, and ownership.

Deferred maintenance and capital plan

Turn inspection notes into a capital plan with timing, cost ranges, responsibility, and business impact. Separate immediate life-safety work from maintenance and future improvements.

Title, survey, and land

Title and survey connect legal rights to physical use. The title commitment lists requirements and exceptions; the ALTA survey shows boundaries, easements, improvements, access, and encroachments.

Review access rights, shared drives, cross-parking, utilities, drainage easements, signage, covenants, and reciprocal easements. A truck entrance across another parcel needs documented rights, not just historical use.

For rail-served property, confirm agreements, switch rights, track maintenance, active service, clearance, and who controls access. Rail value depends on legal and physical service rights matching the marketing description.

Zoning and permits

Confirm zoning district, permitted and special uses, variances, nonconforming rights, outdoor storage, parking, truck routing, screening, signage, and hours. A zoning verification letter can document the municipality's view.

Permits and certificates of occupancy document approved work and uses. Check open permits, violations, expired approvals, fire-code issues, unpermitted mezzanines, and tenant improvements needing closeout.

For local research starting points, use the nine-county hub and municipal records. For Chicago-specific research, see the Chicago commercial real estate guide.

Operational and lease diligence for leased industrial

Verify rent-roll income, obligations, and operating rights against signed documents. Request every lease, amendment, guaranty, side letter, estoppel, SNDA, and occupancy notice.

  • Estoppels: confirm rent, term, deposits, defaults, options, exclusive rights, and tenant claims.
  • SNDAs: coordinate lender, buyer, and tenant rights after a foreclosure or ownership change.
  • CAM reconciliations: test recoveries, caps, exclusions, reserves, management fees, and year-end balances.
  • Repair obligations: identify who pays for roof, structure, HVAC, pavement, dock equipment, fire protection, and capital replacements.
  • Hazardous materials clauses: compare lease rights to actual operations, permits, storage, and environmental reports.
  • Parking and trailer rights: confirm allocated spaces, yard rights, storage limits, and rules for containers or fleet vehicles.

The commercial leasing guide explains why lease labels do not replace a clause-by-clause review.

Utilities and infrastructure

Compare electricity, gas, water, sanitary and storm sewer, compressed air, fiber, and telecom with the user's equipment and occupancy schedule before commitment.

Electrical capacity may involve ComEd, the serving utility, an electrical engineer, and the equipment vendor. Confirm voltage, phase, amperage, transformer ownership, spare capacity, upgrade lead times, and shutdown requirements.

For existing or proposed battery energy storage, collect ownership and service contracts, approved plans, utility permissions, commissioning records, safety documentation and removal obligations. The BESS owner guide explains storage, peak savings, engineered backup, fire and insurance review, and standalone storage land leases. Neither a large service nor nearby transmission guarantees interconnection capacity.

For gas, water, and sewer, check meter size, pressure, discharge limits, floor drains, pretreatment, process water, fire flow, and municipal capacity. General serviceability does not confirm fiber or telecom.

Turning findings into price, credits, escrows, or repairs

Use findings to accept risk, renegotiate, seek credits or escrow, require repairs, modify representations, obtain insurance or terminate where permitted.

Sellers should separate documented defects from buyer preferences and speculative upgrades. Buyers should distinguish routine maintenance from issues affecting value, financing, insurability, or lawful use.

Support material findings with report pages, photos, contractor proposals, municipal responses, title exceptions, lease clauses, or consultant memos.
