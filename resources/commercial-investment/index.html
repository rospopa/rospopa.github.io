Acquisition preparation

Commercial investment and purchase diligence

Educational framework, not an investment recommendation.

By Pavlo Rospopa · Last updated October 10, 2026

In brief

Evaluate Chicago-area commercial or industrial property by function, documented income, financing and capital needs. Price alone shows neither risks nor total cash required.

  • State the purpose first: owner-use, rental income, or redevelopment each needs different underwriting.
  • Underwrite signed leases, collections, expenses, and capital needs, not the marketing summary.
  • Test financing terms and downside cases explicitly; a lender’s approval is not your diligence.
  • Organize legal, zoning, physical, environmental, tax, and tenancy reviews before contract deadlines expire.
  • Owner lens: Review entity authority, partner consent rights, guaranties, capital-call obligations, and loan covenants alongside property economics; a strong asset does not remove ownership-level liquidity risk.

Define the acquisition’s purpose

Owner-users need business fit; investors need documented tenancy and cash flow. Redevelopment adds approval, construction, timing and leasing risks. Define purpose, hold period, capital budget and acceptable downside before comparing.

Compare owner-user costs with a realistic lease alternative, including financing, taxes, insurance, repairs, improvements and committed liquidity. For industrial assets, confirm operational suitability.

Underwrite the documents, not the headline

  • Rent roll and leases: reconcile tenants, occupied areas, rent, deposits, expirations, options, concessions and amendments to signed documents.
  • Collections: separate scheduled from collected rent; identify arrears and disputes.
  • Expenses: verify taxes, insurance, maintenance, management, utilities and recoveries from records, not just the marketing summary.
  • Capital: estimate repairs, replacements, tenant improvements and leasing costs separately; each affects cash needs.
  • Vacancy and rollover: allow for downtime, concessions and replacement-tenant costs.
Net operating income (NOI)
Generally property income less operating expenses, before debt service and income taxes. Capital expenditures and some reserve treatments are usually separate; document the convention in each comparison.
Capitalization rate
NOI divided by price or value on the stated basis. Projected and current documented NOI produce different rates; neither guarantees a return.
Cash flow to equity
Accounts for financing and capital cash needs beyond NOI. Purchase costs, principal repayment, sale costs and taxes also affect the outcome.

Test financing and downside explicitly

Review rate, amortization, maturity, recourse, covenants, reserves, fees, prepayment terms and any balloon payment. Lender approval does not replace your diligence.

DSCR compares defined income with required debt service. Confirm each lender’s definitions and requirements; no universal threshold applies here.

Debt yield, DSCR and refinancing costs

Debt yield divides defined annual NOI by loan balance. Unlike DSCR, rates and amortization do not directly change it, but lender income adjustments matter. LTV uses value; DSCR uses debt service. Multiple tests may limit proceeds; no universal qualifying threshold exists.

Illustrative only: $300,000 NOI / $3,000,000 debt = 10% debt yield. With $240,000 annual debt service, DSCR is 1.25; at an assumed $5,000,000 value, LTV is 60%. These are arithmetic examples, not a Chicago loan quote or approval.

Before refinancing, obtain payoff, prepayment charges, proposed rate, amortization, lender and appraisal fees, escrows and reserves. Calculate proceeds after payoff and costs. Lower appraisals or shorter leases may require new equity despite current debt coverage. Test the gap in the owner budget.

For valuation and cash flow, see the OCC Commercial Real Estate Lending handbook, NOI guide and DCF guide. Property income, equity cash flow and taxable income are different measures.

Organize diligence before deadlines expire

  1. Legal and title: have counsel and title professionals review ownership, exceptions, survey matters, easements, access, contracts, deadlines and remedies.
  2. Use and approvals: confirm zoning, permitted activity, occupancy and building requirements, and approval conditions. See the Chicago property-research checklist.
  3. Physical condition: commission asset-appropriate inspections of the roof, structure, systems, pavement and other material components; turn findings into a timed capital plan.
  4. Environmental: consult an environmental professional on appropriate assessment, historical uses and further investigation. A Phase I is no guarantee that contamination is absent.
  5. Tax and insurance: verify parcel history and obtain advice and quotes for the proposed ownership and use; do not assume seller costs continue.
  6. Tenancy and closing: review leases, amendments, appropriate tenant confirmations, deposits, rent prorations, service contracts and closing documents.

EPA’s All Appropriate Inquiries guidance describes a framework relevant to certain environmental-liability protections. An assessment alone does not establish eligibility; timing, purchaser status and continuing obligations require professional advice.

For industrial acquisitions, the Chicagoland nine-county Illinois hub covers local research beyond Chicago and Cook County. Match each PIN to its county and authority; distinguish current zoning from planned land use before underwriting expansion or a tenant’s operation.

How does CRE investing fit a retirement plan?

Distinguish a taxable investment for future retirement income from property held in an IRA or employer plan. Account ownership changes title, payment paths, borrowing, related-party restrictions and distributions. Property cash must cover debt, capital and leasing costs before funding household spending.

The CRE retirement planning guide compares IRA, Roth, Solo 401(k) and other plans; prohibited transactions and UBIT; and RMD liquidity, diversification, Illinois taxes and succession. Ask qualified tax and plan advisers about eligibility before an offer, loan guarantee or related-business lease.

The federal and Illinois tax guide distinguishes passive from portfolio income and covers cost segregation, straight-line depreciation, lazy 1031, recapture and inherited basis. Underwrite the investment before treating tax deferral as a reason to buy.

Official sources for verification

  • Cook County Assessor and Cook County Treasurer: assessment and tax research for Cook County parcels.
  • EPA: All Appropriate Inquiries: environmental due-diligence background and limitations.
  • FEMA Flood Map Service Center: mapped flood-hazard information.
  • Chicago Department of Buildings: city building and permitting resources.

Review commercial lease terms and obligations when assessing a leased investment or comparing ownership with renting.
