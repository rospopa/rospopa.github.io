Research guide

Researching Chicago commercial real estate

A commercial property search becomes more useful when it starts with a proposed use and a verification plan. An address, an asking price, and a listing description are not enough to establish whether a building can support your business or investment.

Published October 2, 2026 · Educational guide, not a market report.

In brief

  • Start from the proposed use and a verification plan, not from an address or asking price.
  • Identify the municipality, county, and every PIN; the zoning authority follows the parcel, not the mailing address.
  • Zoning maps, building records, and tax bills are starting points that need confirmation with the responsible authority and qualified advisers.
  • Compare options on the same basis: area definition, total cost, timing, and verified evidence.

1. Define what the property must do

Record the intended activity, required occupancy date, usable area, budget, and non-negotiable physical requirements. Separate “must have” constraints from preferences. When considering a property for sale, state whether it is intended for owner occupancy, rental income, or redevelopment; each requires a different underwriting approach.

Office
Consider floor layout, accessibility, telecommunications, commuting, and the cost of adapting space to the team.
Retail and service space
Examine customer access, visibility, delivery arrangements, permitted activity, and any business-specific licensing or buildout requirements.
Industrial and warehouse property
Evaluate loading, clear height, power, circulation, and operations before comparing square footage. Use the industrial evaluation guide.
Flex and mixed-use property
Identify each actual use and its share of the building. A “flex” label does not confirm that manufacturing, retail, laboratory, or assembly activities are permitted.

2. Confirm the jurisdiction and parcel

“Chicago area” can describe the city or a much larger metropolitan region. Identify the municipality, county, address, and every property index number (PIN) involved. A marketing address can represent multiple parcels; a mailing address alone does not establish which zoning authority applies.

  • For a city property, begin with Chicago’s zoning and building resources.
  • For a suburban property, check that municipality’s planning and building departments. If it is unincorporated, confirm the responsible county authority.
  • Use the correct county’s assessor and treasurer. Cook County records should not be assumed to cover property in DuPage, Lake, Will, Kane, or other counties.

Keep a research log: source, retrieval date, parcel or address searched, result, and unresolved question. Retain the underlying documents rather than relying on a screenshot or search snippet.

For industrial property beyond the city, use the nine-county Chicagoland industrial hub. It covers Cook, DeKalb, DuPage, Grundy, Kane, Kendall, Lake, McHenry, and Will counties in Illinois with county-specific planning, zoning, and parcel sources, while keeping municipal authority distinct.

3. Verify use, condition, and obligations

Zoning is a starting point

Locate the zoning designation, then describe the proposed operation to the relevant authority. Confirm whether additional approvals, special uses, parking or loading provisions, or restrictions apply. Existing occupancy and a prior tenant’s business do not automatically establish permission for a new operation.

Building records need interpretation

Review available permit and violation information, then ask qualified advisers to investigate unresolved items. Public records may be incomplete or may not establish the condition of the roof, structure, electrical system, fire protection, or other components. Arrange inspections appropriate to the property and intended use.

Taxes are not a fixed listing number

Match the bill and assessment to the correct PINs and tax year. Distinguish assessed value from a tax bill and from market value. Ask a qualified tax adviser how reassessment, appeals, incentives, and a change in use or ownership could affect the budget; do not assume last year’s bill is the future expense.

Access and environmental questions can change the decision

Check documented access rights, easements, drainage, and flood information. Past industrial activity can warrant environmental diligence even if the present use appears ordinary. The purchase-diligence guide explains how these checks fit into an acquisition.

4. Put each option on the same basis

  • Area: record the source and whether it means rentable, usable, building, or land area.
  • Cost: separate purchase price or base rent from taxes, insurance, maintenance, utilities, improvements, and transaction costs.
  • Timing: account for possession, approvals, construction, and equipment installation—not only the proposed closing or lease date.
  • Evidence: distinguish verified facts from representations and estimates. Asking terms are not proof of completed transactions or market value.

For rental decisions, continue to comparing commercial leases. For an acquisition, continue to investment and purchase diligence.

Official research sources

  • City of Chicago zoning map — initial zoning location research; confirm interpretations with the responsible authority.
  • Chicago Department of Buildings — building permits, inspections, and code-enforcement resources.
  • Cook County Assessor — parcel and assessment research.
  • Cook County Treasurer — tax bills and payment information.
  • FEMA Flood Map Service Center — official flood-hazard mapping; maps do not capture every possible water or drainage problem.
This guide provides a research process, not a determination that any property is legal, safe, available, or suitable. Consult qualified legal, tax, design, and inspection professionals for the specific transaction.
