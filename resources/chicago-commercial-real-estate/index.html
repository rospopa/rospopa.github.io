Research guide

Researching Chicago commercial real estate

A commercial property search becomes more useful when it starts with a proposed use and a verification plan. An address, an asking price, and a listing description are not enough to establish whether a building can support your business or investment.

Educational guide, not a market report.

In brief

  • Start from the proposed use and a verification plan, not from an address or asking price.
  • Identify the municipality, county, and every PIN; the zoning authority follows the parcel, not the mailing address.
  • Zoning maps, building records, and tax bills are starting points that need confirmation with the responsible authority and qualified advisers.
  • Compare options on the same basis: area definition, total cost, timing, and verified evidence.
  • Owner lens: Before spending on a conversion, verify the approval path, who pays for utility or off-site work, and what happens to the budget if approvals or occupancy are delayed.

1. Define what the property must do

Record the proposed activity, occupancy date, usable area, budget and non-negotiable physical requirements. Separate must-haves from preferences. For a property for sale, identify owner occupancy, rental income or redevelopment; each requires different underwriting.

Office
Consider layout, accessibility, telecommunications, commuting and adaptation costs.
Retail and service space
Check customer access, visibility, deliveries, permitted activity, licensing and buildout needs.
Industrial and warehouse property
Evaluate loading, clear height, power, circulation and operations before comparing area. See the industrial evaluation guide.
Flex and mixed-use property
Identify each actual use and its share. “Flex” does not establish permission for manufacturing, retail, laboratory or assembly.

2. Confirm the jurisdiction and parcel

“Chicago area” may mean the city or a much larger region. Identify municipality, county, address and every property index number (PIN). A marketing address may cover multiple parcels; a mailing address does not establish the zoning authority.

  • For city property, start with Chicago zoning and building resources.
  • For suburban property, check municipal planning and building departments; for unincorporated parcels, confirm the county authority.
  • Use the parcel’s county assessor and treasurer. Cook County records do not cover DuPage, Lake, Will, Kane or other counties.

Log the source, retrieval date, parcel or address, result and open questions. Keep source documents, not just screenshots or search snippets.

For industrial property beyond the city, the nine-county Chicagoland industrial hub links county-specific planning, zoning and parcel sources for Cook, DeKalb, DuPage, Grundy, Kane, Kendall, Lake, McHenry and Will counties, while distinguishing municipal authority.

3. Verify use, condition, and obligations

Zoning is a starting point

Find the zoning designation and describe the proposed operation to the authority. Confirm additional approvals, special uses, parking or loading rules and restrictions. Existing occupancy or a prior tenant’s business does not automatically permit a new operation.

Building records need interpretation

Review available permits and violations; have qualified advisers investigate open issues. Public records may be incomplete and do not establish roof, structural, electrical, fire-protection or other conditions. Arrange inspections suited to the property and intended use.

Taxes are not a fixed listing number

Match bills and assessments to the PINs and tax year. Assessed value, tax bill and market value are different. Ask a qualified tax adviser how reassessment, appeals, incentives or a use/ownership change may affect the budget; last year’s bill is not a forecast.

Access and environmental questions can change the decision

Check access rights, easements, drainage and flood information. Past industrial activity may warrant environmental diligence even when current use appears ordinary. The purchase-diligence guide places these checks in acquisition review.

4. Put each option on the same basis

  • Area: record the source and whether it is rentable, usable, building or land area.
  • Cost: separate price or base rent from taxes, insurance, maintenance, utilities, improvements and transaction costs.
  • Timing: include possession, approvals, construction and equipment installation, not just closing or lease dates.
  • Evidence: distinguish verified facts from representations and estimates. Asking terms do not prove a completed transaction or market value.

For rental decisions, continue to comparing commercial leases. For an acquisition, continue to investment and purchase diligence.

Official research sources

  • City of Chicago zoning map: initial zoning location research; confirm interpretations with the responsible authority.
  • Chicago Department of Buildings: building permits, inspections, and code-enforcement resources.
  • Cook County Assessor: parcel and assessment research.
  • Cook County Treasurer: tax bills and payment information.
  • FEMA Flood Map Service Center: official flood-hazard maps, which do not show every water or drainage problem.
This guide provides a research process, not a determination that any property is legal, safe, available, or suitable. Consult qualified legal, tax, design, and inspection professionals for the specific transaction.
