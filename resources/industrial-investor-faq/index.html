Investor Q&A · 10,000 to 100,000 SF industrial

What experienced industrial investors ask, and how to think about the answers

Experienced buyers ask these questions about small and midsize industrial buildings in deal conversations, investor forums and conference panels. The questions are theirs; my answers provide general Chicagoland education.

Educational answers, not advice on any specific property, loan, tax position, or investment.

In brief

  • Price off documented income, then test the hold with explicit rollover, capital, and exit assumptions. Cap rate and IRR answer different questions.
  • Buy for likely tenants: small-bay and bulk distribution needs differ.
  • Underwrite Illinois taxes after sale, not the seller's bill; never assume incentives transfer.
  • Most “surprises” are knowable in diligence: roof, pavement, fire protection, environmental history, zoning conformity, and lease clauses.
  • Sell, refinance, or hold is a capital question first and a market-timing question second.
  • Owner lens: Stress correlated setbacks together, such as tenant loss, tax or insurance increases, a major repair, and tighter refinancing, rather than testing each downside in isolation.

Pricing and returns

Is cap rate or IRR the right way to price a 10,000 to 100,000 SF building?

Cap rate divides documented NOI by price on a stated basis. IRR models income through lease expirations, capital spending, financing and sale. They answer different questions.

Short leases, few tenants, and above- or below-market rents can make a small building’s in-place cap misleading. Price from documented income, then model each lease, including downtime, improvements, commissions, and a defensible exit cap. Try the investment guide’s cap-rate calculator.

How much weight should replacement cost carry?

Replacement cost (land, construction, soft costs, and lease-up) frames downside. Buying well below build cost limits new competition at your rent level, but cost does not set value: low clear height or a cramped truck court can make a building worth less than “cost.”

Use replacement cost to check price per square foot and expected competing supply, not as a target. See building types and specifications for fixable and permanent features.

What does “below-market rent” really mean, and how do I underwrite the mark-to-market?

Only signed comparable leases establish market rent. For small-bay space, suite size, office finish, doors, power, and yard rights narrow the comparison; asking rents are not signed deals.

Rent resets at lease expiration, so model retention probability, downtime, improvements, commissions, and free rent for each expiration. Do not pay for the upside in the price and count it again in the pro forma.

How should I set the exit cap rate and hold period?

Exit cap rate is often the most sensitive assumption; the building will be older at sale. A lower exit than entry cap needs justification, such as a longer lease or completed renovation.

Test a higher exit cap, lower market rent, longer downtime, and delayed sale. If the deal only works in the base case, that case is doing too much work.

Building and functionality

Does clear height matter for buildings this size, or only for bulk distribution?

In 10,000 to 40,000 SF spaces, service companies, contractors, light assembly and last-mile operators may favor drive-in doors, power, office ratio, parking and yard over extra clearance. Distribution and third-party logistics tenants prioritize docks, truck court and racking height.

Buy and underwrite for likely tenants. The building-fit checklist covers tour measurements.

What makes a Class B or C building “functionally obsolete,” and can capital fix it?

Capital can fix roofs, lighting, dock equipment, pavement, offices, electrical service, and sometimes add dock positions. It cannot fix low clear height, tight columns, a shallow truck court, high site coverage without expansion land, or poor access. Those limits cap rent and narrow the buyer pool.

Price permanent limitations. A well-located, functional Class B building can still be durable if it serves local demand and is hard to replace.

How do I value excess land or an outdoor storage yard attached to a building?

Confirm zoning and approvals for outdoor storage, trailer parking or equipment staging. Verify access, surfacing, lighting, drainage, stormwater compliance and separate leaseability. Even industrial districts may restrict yard use.

Permitted, separately leasable yard is worth more than yard that is merely tolerated. The nine-county hub links zoning authorities.

Multi-tenant or single-tenant: which is less risky in this size range?

Multi-tenant buildings spread vacancy risk but bring more frequent rollover, demising, metering, and management. Single-tenant buildings are simpler to own and finance but carry binary vacancy risk tied to one company.

Choose the risk you can manage. If you cannot lease small suites efficiently, diversification on paper becomes vacancy in practice.

Tenants and leases

How do I underwrite tenant credit when the tenants are private companies?

Request what the lease allows: financial statements, payment history, time in business, ownership, guaranties, and security. Also assess durability: heavy equipment, permits, customer proximity, and specialized build-outs make moving costly.

In small-bay buildings, re-leasing ease may matter more than one tenant’s credit. A functional suite in a liquid submarket is its own credit support.

Which lease clauses most affect value?

  • Expense pass-throughs, especially whether real estate taxes after a reassessment are recoverable and whether CAM is capped.
  • Roof, structure, HVAC, and pavement obligations: who replaces, not just who repairs.
  • Renewal options at fixed or capped rents, which can cap your mark-to-market.
  • Termination, contraction, assignment, and sublease rights.
  • Hazardous materials, outdoor storage, trailer parking, and hours clauses that govern how the property may actually be used.
  • Holdover, security, and guaranty terms.

Confirm all of it with tenant estoppels before closing. The lease-review checklist covers the mechanics.

How do I underwrite rollover and downtime realistically?

Model each lease expiration instead of using a blended vacancy factor. Assign retention probability, downtime, improvement and commission costs, and free rent; then test simultaneous departures, lower renewal rent, and longer lease-up.

For concentrated rollover, consider negotiating early renewals or extensions. Sellers may also value certainty.

In a sale-leaseback, what am I really buying?

The seller-tenant’s ability to pay during the lease and the real estate if it stops. Rent may reflect the seller’s financing need, not the market; compare it with market rent to assess residual risk.

Weigh lease term, guaranties, reporting covenants, and maintenance alongside price. The selling guide explains the owner-user’s perspective.

Diligence and risk

When is a Phase I enough, and when do I need a Phase II?

Phase I identifies recognized environmental conditions through records, interviews and a site visit, without sampling. Phase II samples soil, groundwater or vapor when Phase I, site history or surrounding uses indicate risk. Lenders frequently require it for flagged conditions.

Allow time for sampling and lab work. Understand the Illinois EPA Site Remediation Program and no-further-remediation letters before relying on them. The due diligence guide covers the process; EPA’s All Appropriate Inquiries page explains the federal framework.

Which physical items most often become price adjustments?

Roof age and warranty, pavement and truck court, dock equipment, office HVAC, and electrical capacity. For storage users, confirm sprinkler adequacy for the next tenant’s commodities and rack heights; upgrades can be expensive.

A property condition assessment schedules these costs and supports negotiations.

If the building is already operating, how much does zoning conformity matter?

It matters for changes of use, expansion, outdoor storage or different tenants. A legal nonconforming use may continue but not expand or restart after a lapse. Obtain the zoning designation and written municipal confirmation of planned uses.

What should I know about flood and stormwater for Chicago-area industrial sites?

Check the FEMA flood map first, then the local stormwater rules: in Cook County the Metropolitan Water Reclamation District administers a watershed management ordinance, and the collar counties and municipalities have their own requirements. Detention obligations can affect expansion plans and yard surfacing, and a flat, paved industrial site is not immune to drainage problems.

Illinois taxes and incentives

Will my purchase trigger a reassessment, and how do I underwrite it?

A recorded Illinois sale can inform assessed value. Cook County reassesses by region every three years; collar counties use their own cycles with annual equalization. Underwrite your likely post-sale tax bill, not the seller’s, and confirm leases allow recovery of increases.

Build an appeal calendar during diligence. The property tax guide links all nine county assessment offices.

Does a Cook County Class 6b incentive stay with the property when it sells?

Do not assume transfer. Check current Cook County Assessor bulletins for remaining reduced assessments, ownership or occupancy change requirements, renewal resolutions and termination conditions. Underwrite with and without the incentive; treat the difference as negotiable risk.

How does a TIF district affect an industrial owner?

You pay taxes normally; TIF redirects the increment above a base to public purposes, often under redevelopment agreements. Check binding property agreements, district expiration and dependent incentives in governing documents, not the map legend.

Debt and capital

How do lenders size loans on small industrial buildings, and what trips borrowers up?

Lenders test loan-to-value, debt service coverage, and often debt yield using their own income and expense definitions. Common hurdles include leases shorter than the loan (requiring rollover reserves or structure), environmental findings, appraisal gaps, recourse, and prepayment limits.

Confirm definitions early and model the lender’s case. The debt-service calculator shows the arithmetic.

How should I think about refinancing risk at loan maturity?

Test whether the property still carries itself with a higher rate, lower loan amount, and tougher appraisal. Usual defenses include moderate leverage, staggered maturities, extension options, and a capital plan independent of cash-out proceeds.

When does SBA 504 financing matter to an investor?

Owner-users compete for the same buildings. A company occupying most of a building can often use the SBA 504 program's long-term fixed-rate component to justify a price beyond rental income value. Expect this competition for vacant or soon-vacant buildings, not long-leased investments.

Timing, exit, and sourcing

How do I decide between selling, refinancing, and holding?

Start with upcoming rollover, roof or pavement costs, loan maturity, tax basis, an exchange plan, and liquidity. Then ask whether you would buy the building today at its potential sale price. If not, reconsider the hold.

The selling guide covers buyer questions and the sale process.

Who is the likely buyer of my building at exit?

For 10,000 to 100,000 SF buildings, likely buyers include owner-users, private investors, exchange buyers, and smaller funds. Size, condition, and leases shape the pool: vacant functional buildings attract owner-users; leased buildings with term attract investors and exchange buyers. Prepare the document file for likely buyers.

How are off-market deals really sourced?

Through owner outreach, broker networks, estates or partnerships in transition, and lease expirations. Be fundable and ready, but remember “off-market” can mean mispriced either way. Underwrite it like any other deal.
