DCF explained for commercial real estate owners

Educational, not an appraisal or investment, tax, legal, engineering or insurance advice. Verify your property, leases and current rules with qualified advisers.

By Pavlo Rospopa · Last updated October 10, 2026

In brief

Discounted cash flow (DCF) values future property receipts and costs today, exposing lease rollover, uneven capital work and changes hidden by cap-rate snapshots.

  • Project income, expenses and capital cash needs year by year.
  • An exit cap rate converts future NOI into a resale estimate; a discount rate converts future cash into present value.
  • Unlevered cash flows exclude debt; levered equity cash flows include it.
  • Sensitivity is more useful than a single optimistic IRR.
  • Owner lens: For an owner-user, separate the building investment from the operating business: compare ownership with a realistic lease alternative and include moving, downtime, fit-out, and eventual disposition costs.

How do you build a property cash-flow projection?

Set valuation date and hold period. Project tenant rent, options and expirations; separate recoveries, taxes, insurance, repairs and management. Model rollover retention or vacancy, downtime, free rent, supported new rent, tenant improvements (TI) and leasing commissions (LC). Do not count the same vacancy loss in reduced rent and a separate deduction.

Property cash flow is more than NOI: subtract capital replacements and leasing costs when incurred, and state reserve treatment. In a 10,000-100,000-SF building, one tenant departure or roof project can dominate a year. Use condition reports and lease abstracts, not a universal rent-growth rate.

Unlevered cash flow excludes financing. Equity models add acquisition loan proceeds, subtract hold-period debt service and deduct sale payoff and finance charges. Match discount rates and return labels to cash flows; separate pre-tax and after-tax models.

Discount rate versus cap rate: what is the difference?

Discount rates value future cash today at an assumed required return. Cap rates divide one year’s income by value. Exit caps assume resale, commonly using next-year stabilized NOI at hold’s end. These rates are neither interchangeable nor necessarily equal.

For end-of-year cash flow, present value = cash flow / (1 + discount rate)year. Net present value (NPV) subtracts the initial investment from discounted receipts. Positive NPV means the modeled investment exceeds the chosen return requirement on these assumptions, not that the outcome is certain.

IRR is the rate that makes NPV zero. Later negative outlays can produce multiple or unhelpful IRRs; dated cash flows also differ from simple annual periods. Keep NPV, required capital and downside exposure visible even when IRR looks attractive.

DCF worked example: operating cash, reversion and present value

Illustration only, not market data, an appraisal or an investment recommendation. Assumptions: three-year unlevered hold, end-of-year receipts, no income taxes and an 8% discount rate.

Assumed annual dollars; capital and leasing cash outlays below NOI
YearNOICapital / TI / LCOperating cash
1$200,000$50,000$150,000
2$210,000$30,000$180,000
3$220,000$30,000$190,000

Assume year-4 NOI of $230,000 and a 7% exit cap. Gross year-3 reversion = $230,000 / 0.07 = $3,285,714.29; after assumed 2% sale costs, net reversion is $3,220,000. Year-3 total cash is $3,410,000, including $190,000 operating cash. Year-4 NOI prices the sale but is not received again during the hold.

Present value = $150,000 / 1.08 + $180,000 / 1.082 + $3,410,000 / 1.083 = $3,000,177.82. With $3,000,000 all-in initial investment, NPV is $177.82; at $3,100,000, NPV is negative $99,822.18. Results depend entirely on assumptions and cash timing.

How do exit cap, rent and downtime change the result?

At $230,000 year-4 NOI, illustrative gross resale is $3,833,333 at a 6% exit cap, $3,285,714 at 7% and $2,875,000 at 8%, rounded to dollars before sale costs. A higher exit cap lowers resale at the same income. None is a Chicago market claim.

Model discount/exit-rate combinations and a separate operating downside: lower collected rent, higher tax and insurance, longer downtime, larger TI/LC, capital overruns and delayed sale. Rent growth and a lower exit cap are not guaranteed benefits. In a levered case, include refinancing maturity and required additional equity, not just revised return.

Direct capitalization is a useful NOI/cap-rate snapshot for stabilized income. DCF exposes changes and execution costs, but detail cannot make speculative assumptions reliable. Have an appraiser and financial/tax advisers review property-specific inputs and conventions.

Owner questions and answers

Is the discount rate the same as the cap rate?

No. A discount rate values a series of future cash flows; a cap rate relates one year of NOI to value. An exit cap rate is a future resale assumption.

Does a high IRR prove a good real estate investment?

No. Cash timing and assumptions drive IRR; nonconventional flows can mislead. It does not measure absolute capital at risk. Review NPV, required equity and downside.

Should DCF include mortgage payments?

Include debt service and loan payoff in a levered equity model, not in an unlevered property model. Match the discount rate to the cash-flow basis.
