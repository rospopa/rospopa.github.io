Industrial building types and specifications

A guide to industrial building types and the specifications buyers, tenants, lenders, and advisors review for 10,000 to 100,000 square foot Chicagoland assets.

In brief

  • Loading, clear height, power, truck movement, fire protection, and zoning determine utility, not the building label.
  • Fit depends on operations, storage, labor, and equipment; a building suited to one user may not suit another.
  • Class A, B, and C are informal labels. Tie them to verifiable features.
  • Confirm specifications through documents, measurements, municipal records, and qualified professionals.
  • A clear seller spec sheet can reduce re-trading and reach the right buyer or tenant pool.
  • Owner lens: Prioritize costly-to-change features such as clear height, column grid, truck court, power, fire protection, and legal yard use over cosmetic fit-out when weighing long-term adaptability.

Why type and spec matter for 10,000 to 100,000 SF buildings

Buyers in the 10,000 to 100,000 square foot range include owner-users and private investors; tenants are often local or regional. The same building may be an operating facility, income investment, or re-tenanting opportunity.

Owner-users assess doors, power, production flow, office area, parking, and expansion room. Tenants compare occupancy cost, timing, lease obligations, and operational disruption. Investors review the lease and potential replacement users.

The industrial building evaluation checklist and seller guide focus on function, records, and verifiable facts.

  • Function: Does the building support the use without unusual workarounds?
  • Flexibility: Could another user occupy it if the first plan changes?
  • Risk: Are there physical, zoning, environmental, or utility constraints that affect timing or cost?

Specifications explained and why each matters

Explain each specification's practical effect and separate measured facts from assumptions or broker shorthand.

Clear height
Clear height is usually measured from the finished floor to the lowest obstruction, not the roof deck. It affects racking, equipment, lighting, and sprinklers; check beams, joists, ducts, heaters, fans, and piping.
Column spacing and speed bays
Spacing affects storage, forklift movement, production cells, and demising. Speed bays can add dock staging depth; verify dimensions and obstructions.
Dock-high doors and door ratio
Dock-high loading supports trailer-height movement, often critical for distribution. Door-to-area ratios compare loading intensity, but the right ratio depends on operations, trailer dwell, staffing, and scheduling.
Drive-in doors
These admit vehicles, equipment, or vans at grade. Verify clear opening, apron slope, condition, security, and safe use across grade changes.
Dock levelers, seals, shelters, and bumpers
These affect safety, weather protection, handling, and efficiency. Review condition, capacity, controls, and maintenance records.
Truck court depth and turning
Circulation depends on court depth, trailer length, door angle, opposing parking, curbs, fences, and stored materials. Test the site plan against the intended truck using the truck dimensions and turning-path reference to distinguish body, cargo, and overall dimensions and prepare a fleet-specific swept-path review.
Trailer and car parking
Trailer, car, fleet, and visitor spaces serve different functions. Verify striping, easements, shared rights, zoning, and outdoor-storage permissions.
Slab thickness, flatness, and load
Slab performance affects racking, forklifts, equipment, and high-bay storage. Relevant ACI and ASTM concepts do not replace a structural engineer's review of thickness, joints, flatness, condition, and material load capacity.
Power
Record amps, voltage, phase, panels, switchgear, transformer location, spare capacity, and utility service. Three-phase power often matters for machinery, compressors, welders, refrigeration, charging, and process loads.
Lighting
Lighting affects safety, productivity, and energy cost. Check fixture type, controls, exterior and emergency lighting, and measured levels where needed.
Fire protection
Wet-pipe systems, ESFR sprinklers, in-rack sprinklers, pumps, and alarms support different storage designs. A fire-protection engineer and the authority having jurisdiction should review commodity, storage height, rack layout, and NFPA 13.
Roof
Type, age, warranty, drainage, penetrations, insulation, and repair history inform capital planning. A roof consultant can distinguish maintenance from near-term replacement risk.
HVAC, ventilation, and warehouse heat
Industrial HVAC often varies across office, production, and warehouse areas. Check heaters, make-up air, exhaust, ventilation, cooling, controls, and proposed-use code requirements.
Office finish percentage
Office area may suit administrative users but reduce warehouse depth. Confirm rentable area, quality, HVAC, restrooms, demising walls, and conversion cost.
Floor-to-floor height for mezzanines
Mezzanines and two-level offices depend on clearances, structure, stairs, egress, accessibility, and fire protection. Verify permits, load rating, and inclusion in reported square footage.
Site coverage and expansion land
Low site coverage may leave room for yard, parking, or expansion, but zoning, setbacks, stormwater, utilities, easements, and wetlands can constrain use. Vacant land is not necessarily buildable.
Zoning and permitted use
Zoning controls uses, outdoor storage, parking, signage, truck traffic, hours, and nonconforming rights. Confirm the intended use with the municipality, not just the district name.

For safety and workplace requirements, start with applicable code officials and qualified professionals; federal OSHA materials may also be relevant to operations.

Class A, B, and C as informal labels

Industrial class labels are market shorthand, not a single official standard. They can be useful only when tied to facts that can be inspected and documented.

Informal ways people describe industrial building class
LabelCommon descriptionCaution
Class ANewer or recently modernized building with stronger loading, height, systems, truck circulation, life-safety features, and institutional-quality records.A small Class A building may still be wrong for a user if doors, power, or zoning do not fit.
Class BFunctional building with some older features, moderate office finish, usable loading, and a condition profile that may require selective capital work.Class B can be a broad category, so list the actual strengths and weaknesses.
Class COlder or more specialized building with lower clear height, limited loading, heavier deferred maintenance, or narrower reuse.Class C does not mean unusable; it means the buyer pool may be more specific and diligence may be more important.

How specs translate to buyer and tenant pools

Specifications determine who can operate efficiently. Strong doors, turning, power, and fire protection may broaden distribution or manufacturing demand compared with a similarly sized building with limited utility.

Functional obsolescence is a mismatch with current operating needs, such as low clear height, shallow truck courts, insufficient power, limited parking, excess office finish, difficult column spacing, or loading unsuited to building size.

Investors assess re-tenanting risk by estimating the potential user pool, improvement timing, and required capital. See the investment diligence guide and commercial leasing guide.

  • Broader pool: flexible layout, adequate power, code-compliant improvements, documented systems, and common loading patterns.
  • Narrower pool: very specialized equipment, unusual floor plan, unresolved environmental history, nonconforming yard, or limited truck access.
  • Seller takeaway: disclose constraints clearly and support strengths with records rather than adjectives.
