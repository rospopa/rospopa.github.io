Industrial building types and specifications

This guide explains common industrial building types and the physical specifications buyers, tenants, lenders, and advisors review for 10,000 to 100,000 square foot assets in Chicagoland.

Published October 3, 2026 · Educational, not an engineering report or valuation.

In brief

  • Industrial labels are shorthand; the actual utility comes from loading, clear height, power, truck movement, fire protection, and zoning.
  • A building can be excellent for one user and inefficient for another because operations, storage profile, labor, and equipment differ.
  • Class A, B, and C descriptions are informal and should be tied to specific, verifiable features rather than marketing labels.
  • Specifications should be confirmed from documents, site measurements, municipal records, and qualified professionals.
  • For sellers, a clear spec sheet can reduce re-trading and help match the right buyer or tenant pool.

Why type and spec matter for 10,000 to 100,000 SF buildings

In the 10,000 to 100,000 square foot range, many assets are bought by owner-users, private investors, and local or regional tenants. The same building may be evaluated as an operating facility, an income investment, or a future re-tenanting opportunity.

Owner-users often start with fit: doors, power, production flow, office area, parking, and expansion room. Tenants compare total occupancy cost, timing, lease obligations, and whether the site can support their operations without major disruption.

Investors review the current lease profile and then ask who else could use the building if the tenant leaves. That is why the industrial building evaluation checklist and the seller file described in the selling guide focus on function, records, and verifiable facts.

  • Function: Does the building support the use without unusual workarounds?
  • Flexibility: Could another user occupy it if the first plan changes?
  • Risk: Are there physical, zoning, environmental, or utility constraints that affect timing or cost?

Specifications explained and why each matters

A useful spec sheet explains both the number and the practical effect. Where possible, separate measured facts from assumptions or broker shorthand.

Clear height
Clear height is commonly measured from the finished floor to the lowest obstruction, not simply to the roof deck. It affects racking, equipment, lighting, and sprinkler design, and it should be checked against beams, joists, ducts, heaters, fans, and sprinkler piping.
Column spacing and speed bays
Column spacing affects storage layout, forklift movement, production cells, and future demising. Speed bays near docks can provide staging depth for loading, but verify actual dimensions and obstructions.
Dock-high doors and door ratio
Dock-high loading supports trailer-height movement and can be critical for distribution. Door-to-area ratios are a concept for comparing loading intensity, but the right ratio depends on the operation, trailer dwell, staffing, and truck scheduling.
Drive-in doors
Drive-in doors allow vehicles, equipment, or vans to enter at grade. Verify clear opening, apron slope, door condition, security, and whether grade changes affect safe use.
Dock levelers, seals, shelters, and bumpers
These components affect safety, weather protection, product handling, and dock efficiency. Condition, capacity, controls, and maintenance records should be reviewed during physical diligence.
Truck court depth and turning
Truck circulation depends on court depth, trailer length, door angle, opposing parking, curbs, fences, and stored materials. A site plan should be tested against the intended truck type.
Trailer and car parking
Trailer stalls, car stalls, fleet spaces, and visitor spaces serve different functions. Verify striping, easements, shared rights, zoning requirements, and whether outdoor storage is permitted.
Slab thickness, flatness, and load
Slab performance matters for racking, forklifts, equipment, and high-bay storage. Concepts from ACI and ASTM standards may be relevant, but a structural engineer should verify thickness, joints, flatness, condition, and load capacity when material.
Power
Record amps, voltage, phase, panels, switchgear, transformer location, spare capacity, and utility service. Three-phase power is often important for machinery, compressors, welders, refrigeration, and certain charging or process loads.
Lighting
Lighting affects safety, productivity, and energy cost. Verify fixture type, controls, exterior lighting, emergency lighting, and measured light levels where operations require them.
Fire protection
Wet-pipe systems, ESFR sprinklers, in-rack sprinklers, pumps, and alarms each support different storage designs. Commodity classification, storage height, rack layout, and the referenced NFPA 13 standard should be reviewed by a fire-protection engineer and the authority having jurisdiction.
Roof
Roof type, age, remaining warranty, drainage, penetrations, insulation, and repair history affect capital planning. A roof consultant can separate routine maintenance from near-term replacement risk.
HVAC, ventilation, and warehouse heat
Industrial HVAC is often uneven across office, production, and warehouse areas. Verify heaters, make-up air, exhaust, ventilation, cooling, controls, and code requirements for the proposed use.
Office finish percentage
Office area can help administrative users and hurt users that need warehouse depth. Confirm rentable area, quality, HVAC service, restrooms, demising walls, and conversion cost.
Floor-to-floor height for mezzanines
Mezzanines and two-level office areas depend on clearances, structure, stairs, egress, accessibility, and fire protection. Verify permits, load rating, and whether the mezzanine is included in reported square footage.
Site coverage and expansion land
Low site coverage may allow yard, parking, or expansion, but zoning, setbacks, stormwater, utilities, easements, and wetlands can limit usable land. Do not assume vacant land is buildable.
Zoning and permitted use
Zoning controls uses, outdoor storage, parking, signage, truck traffic, hours, and nonconforming rights. Confirm the actual intended use with the municipality rather than relying only on a zoning district name.

For safety and workplace requirements, start with applicable code officials and qualified professionals; federal OSHA materials may also be relevant to operations.

Class A, B, and C as informal labels

Industrial class labels are market shorthand, not a single official standard. They can be useful only when tied to facts that can be inspected and documented.

Informal ways people describe industrial building class
LabelCommon descriptionCaution
Class ANewer or recently modernized building with stronger loading, height, systems, truck circulation, life-safety features, and institutional-quality records.A small Class A building may still be wrong for a user if doors, power, or zoning do not fit.
Class BFunctional building with some older features, moderate office finish, usable loading, and a condition profile that may require selective capital work.Class B can be a broad category, so list the actual strengths and weaknesses.
Class COlder or more specialized building with lower clear height, limited loading, heavier deferred maintenance, or narrower reuse.Class C does not mean unusable; it means the buyer pool may be more specific and diligence may be more important.

How specs translate to buyer and tenant pools

Specifications shape demand because they determine who can operate efficiently. A property with strong doors, turning, power, and fire protection may appeal to a broader distribution or manufacturing audience than one with the same square footage but limited utility.

Functional obsolescence occurs when older features reduce usefulness compared with current operating needs. Examples include low clear height, shallow truck courts, insufficient power, limited parking, heavy office finish, difficult column spacing, or loading that does not match the building size.

Investors consider re-tenanting risk by asking how many users can realistically occupy the space, how long improvements might take, and what capital would be needed. For income-property context, see the investment diligence guide and the commercial leasing guide.

  • Broader pool: flexible layout, adequate power, code-compliant improvements, documented systems, and common loading patterns.
  • Narrower pool: very specialized equipment, unusual floor plan, unresolved environmental history, nonconforming yard, or limited truck access.
  • Seller takeaway: disclose constraints clearly and support strengths with records rather than adjectives.

A spec sheet should help a buyer, tenant, lender, or inspector confirm facts quickly. If a detail is unknown, mark it as to be verified rather than guessing.
