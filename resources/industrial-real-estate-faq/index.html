  • Industrial real estate is property built to make, store, move, or service goods, including warehouses, distribution centers, manufacturing plants, flex buildings, and outdoor storage yards.

Industrial real estate basics

What is industrial real estate?

Industrial real estate supports manufacturing, storage, distribution and physical goods services: warehouses, fulfillment centers, plants, flex buildings, cold storage, truck terminals and outdoor yards. Unlike office or retail, value depends on function (clear height, loading, truck access, power) and access to highways, rail, labor and customers. See industrial building types and specifications.

What are the main types of industrial property?

Types include bulk warehouse and distribution, light industrial and small-bay, manufacturing, flex/R&D, cold storage, truck terminals and cross-docks, data centers, specialized facilities and industrial outdoor storage (IOS). Tenant needs and underwriting differ, so specifications matter more than labels. The building types guide compares them.

What is the difference between a warehouse and a distribution center?

A warehouse mainly stores goods; a distribution center moves them quickly. Inventory turnover in days rather than months typically requires more docks, deeper truck courts, trailer parking, employees and conveyors. Many buildings suit either use; dock count, court depth and parking determine fit.

What is flex space?

Flex combines finished office or showroom space (often a quarter or more) with warehouse, lab or light assembly space. Usually lower and with more grade-level doors than a warehouse, it suits companies needing offices and goods together. See flex space in the glossary.

What is small-bay industrial?

Small-bay (or shallow-bay) buildings have suites of a few thousand to roughly 25,000 square feet for contractors, service businesses, distributors and light manufacturers. Suites typically include an office, drive-in or dock door and separate utilities. Limited new construction supports a deep local market for 10,000 to 100,000 SF properties.

What is a last-mile industrial facility?

A last-mile (or last-touch) warehouse or service building serves final deliveries near dense population. Customer, labor and arterial road access, plus van and fleet parking, outweigh size or height. Chicago and inner-ring suburban infill sites are typical locations.

What is industrial outdoor storage (IOS)?

IOS land provides truck and trailer parking, equipment staging or material storage, usually fenced, lit, paved or graveled with little or no building. Value depends on zoning, access, surfacing, stormwater compliance and scarcity; many municipalities restrict it. See IOS in the glossary.

What is cold storage, and why is it different?

Cold storage is a temperature-controlled warehouse for refrigerated or frozen goods. Insulated panels, refrigeration systems, freezer-grade floors with under-slab heating, heavy power, and specialized docks make it far more expensive to build and convert than a dry warehouse. That cost, plus food-safety requirements, keeps supply limited and tenants sticky.

What do Class A, B, and C mean for industrial buildings?

Class describes age and functionality: A is newer, with modern clear heights, ESFR sprinklers, deep truck courts and wide columns; B is older but functional; C has limits such as low clear height, tight columns or poor access. Classes are not standardized; compare specifications. See industrial building classes.

Who leases and owns industrial buildings?

Tenants include manufacturers, distributors, third-party logistics (3PL), e-commerce and retail companies, contractors, food and beverage firms and service businesses. Owners include occupying owner-users, private investors, family offices, syndicators, private equity funds, institutions and publicly traded REITs. Owner-users and private investors are the most common 10,000 to 100,000 SF buyers.

Building specifications

What is clear height in a warehouse?

Clear height is the distance from the floor to the lowest overhead obstruction, usually roof joists, beams, or sprinkler lines. It sets racking height and storage capacity. Measure the lowest point, not the roof peak. See clear height in the glossary.

How much clear height does a modern warehouse need?

Modern bulk distribution commonly has mid-30s to 40 feet clear height; functional small-bay and older buildings often have 16 to 24 feet. Racked distribution benefits from height; contractors, service users and light manufacturers may prioritize doors, power and yard. Match likely tenants.

What is the difference between dock-high and grade-level doors?

A dock-high door aligns with truck-bed height (roughly four feet above the court), letting forklifts enter semi-trailers. A grade-level (drive-in) door lets vehicles and equipment enter from the ground. Distribution users need docks; contractors and service users often need drive-in doors. Many buildings have both. See dock-high door and drive-in door.

How many dock doors does a warehouse need?

A common distribution rule of thumb is one dock door per 5,000 to 10,000 square feet; cross-dock or parcel operations may need more, long-term storage fewer. Count usable doors with working levelers, seals, and trailer positions, not wall openings.

What is a truck court, and how deep should it be?

The truck court is the paved maneuvering and parking area before dock doors. Modern single-sided courts commonly measure 120 to 135 feet, with more depth needed when trucks use both sides. Shallow courts limit truck access and are hard to fix. See truck court.

These are screening examples, not safe-clearance guarantees. Review the sourced vehicle dimensions and turning-path reference and have a qualified engineer check the fleet's entry, backing, and exit paths.

Why does column spacing matter in an industrial building?

Column spacing affects how efficiently racking, aisles, and equipment fit. Wider spacing adds flexibility; tight spacing wastes floor area and may rule out users. Modern buildings commonly use 50-foot bays with deeper speed bays at docks. See column spacing.

What is an ESFR sprinkler system?

ESFR (early suppression, fast response) is a ceiling sprinkler system designed to suppress high-piled storage fires without in-rack sprinklers in many configurations. Adequacy depends on commodity, storage and building height, and water supply; a fire-protection engineer should confirm fit for each tenant. See ESFR sprinklers.

How much electrical power does an industrial building need?

Lighting and battery charging may need modest service; manufacturing, cold storage, and EV fleets may need much more. Service is described by amperage, voltage, and phase; three-phase is standard for motors and machinery. Confirm capacity with an electrician and utility, as upgrades can take months. See three-phase power.

What should I know about the floor slab?

Racking, forklifts and machinery depend on slab thickness, reinforcement, capacity and flatness. Tall racks and narrow-aisle equipment need level floors; heavy manufacturing needs high capacity. Review drawings, cracking and joint failure; have an engineer assess demanding uses. See slab load and flatness.

How much office space is typical in an industrial building?

Warehouse and distribution buildings often have under 10 to 15 percent office; flex buildings may have more. Excess office raises costs, may sit empty, and narrows the tenant pool. Match office area to local users. See office finish percentage.

Industrial leases

How do industrial leases usually work?

Most industrial leases are net: base rent plus tenants' share of taxes, insurance and common-area or maintenance costs. Terms govern rent, length, use, repairs, renewal and move-out. Compare total occupancy cost, not just base rent. See the lease comparison guide.

What is a triple-net (NNN) lease?

A triple-net lease requires tenants to pay base rent plus their share of property taxes, insurance, and common-area maintenance. It is common for industrial property, but leases differ on roof, structure, parking, and HVAC replacements. Read repair and capital clauses. See NNN in the glossary.

What is the difference between gross, modified gross, and NNN leases?

In a gross lease, the landlord covers expenses through higher rent. Modified gross splits expenses, such as utilities and increases above a base year. In an NNN lease, tenants pay their share of taxes, insurance, and maintenance in addition to base rent. Compare all-in annual cost. See gross and modified gross.

What are CAM charges?

CAM (common area maintenance) is a tenant’s share of shared-property costs such as snow removal, landscaping, parking repair, lighting, and sometimes management. Check inclusions, allocations, caps, and capital pass-throughs. See CAM in the glossary.

How long are industrial leases?

Industrial leases commonly run three to ten years: often three to five for small-bay and small-business tenants, ten or more for large distribution and build-to-suit. Longer terms usually bring more landlord-funded improvements and free rent; shorter terms offer flexibility. Renewal options let tenants extend.

How do rent escalations work in industrial leases?

Base rent usually rises by a fixed percentage or amount per square foot; some leases use CPI or stepped increases. Escalations compound, so model total rent over the term. See escalations.

What is a tenant improvement (TI) allowance?

A TI allowance funds tenant build-out or modifications such as offices, electrical, lighting, restrooms, or dock work. Usually quoted per square foot, it reflects lease term and credit and is recovered through rent. Clarify construction management and treatment of unused funds. See TI allowance.

How is industrial rent quoted?

Industrial rent is usually annual dollars per square foot, net of taxes, insurance and CAM. Smaller suites may quote monthly gross rent. Confirm net or gross, annual or monthly, and request current expense estimates.

Who pays for the roof, HVAC, and structural repairs in an industrial lease?

The lease controls. Tenants commonly maintain their space and HVAC; landlords retain roof replacement and structure. Some “absolute NNN” leases shift everything to tenants. Distinguish repair and replacement duties and whether replacements can be amortized into CAM.

What should I ask before leasing a warehouse?

Confirm zoning allows your use; clear height, door types and count, truck-court depth, and trailer parking; electrical service; sprinkler adequacy for your storage; office and restroom count; current taxes, insurance, and CAM; repair and replacement responsibilities; renewal and expansion rights; restoration obligations at move-out; and access hours, security, and outdoor storage rights. The tour checklist covers what to measure.

Space, rent, and lease-or-buy

How much warehouse space do I need?

Start with inventory, pallets, racking height, aisles, staging, shipping, equipment, office headcount and growth, plus truck, parking and yard needs. A racking or logistics consultant's efficient layout may need less space than a rough estimate.

How much does it cost to lease industrial space in the Chicago area?

Chicagoland rent varies by submarket, size, age, clear height, office share, and yard. Small suites and infill locations generally cost more per square foot than large outer-corridor buildings. Budget for taxes, insurance, and CAM, which can be significant in Cook County. Check quarterly brokerage reports and signed comparable leases, not just asking rents.

What factors drive industrial lease rates?

Location and access, vacancy in the submarket, building size and suite size, clear height, docks and doors, truck court and trailer parking, power, office finish, age and condition, outdoor storage rights, lease term, tenant credit, and improvement allowances. Property taxes also matter because tenants weigh total occupancy cost, not just base rent.

Should a business lease or buy its industrial building?

Buying offers control, equity, rent-increase protection and a long-term asset, but ties up capital and adds concentration and repair risk. Leasing preserves cash and flexibility. Stable businesses needing specialized improvements may buy with SBA 504 financing; fast-growing firms often lease. Compare the same horizon with your accountant.

What is a build-to-suit?

A build-to-suit serves a specific tenant or buyer, usually committed before construction. A long lease or purchase commitment secures needed specifications and reduces developer leasing risk. Site selection, entitlements, design and construction often take a year or more.

What is a speculative (spec) industrial building?

A speculative (spec) building anticipates demand without a signed tenant. Generic modern standards suit many users and permit faster occupancy than build-to-suit, while added supply can affect submarket rents.

What is an industrial sublease, and is it a good option?

A sublease offers some or all of a tenant's space, usually with landlord consent. It may cost less and include furniture or racking, but cannot exceed the original term and depends on the tenant's good standing. Read the master lease.

What security deposit or guaranty will an industrial landlord ask for?

Security reflects tenant finances, time in business, lease term, and landlord costs for improvements and commissions. Expect a cash deposit or letter of credit; newer or smaller tenants may also need a personal or corporate guaranty. Negotiate a burn-down after on-time payments.

Who pays the broker on an industrial lease?

Landlords usually pay leasing commissions under their listing agreement, typically sharing them with the tenant's broker. Arrangements vary; confirm each broker's compensation and required Illinois agency disclosures. See leasing commission.

What is a letter of intent (LOI) in commercial real estate?

An LOI summarizes price or rent, term, contingencies, timing and conditions before the purchase agreement or lease. Business terms usually do not bind, but confidentiality or exclusivity may. Clear terms reduce surprises. See LOI.

Investing in industrial

Is industrial real estate a good investment?

Industrial has performed strongly over the past decade, supported by e-commerce, supply-chain changes, and limited infill supply. Buildings are relatively simple to operate and often net-leased, but returns depend on price, rents, lease terms, rates, and new construction. No property is a good investment at any price. See the investment guide.

What are the advantages of investing in industrial property?

Advantages can include net leases that pass through operating costs, lower capital spending per square foot than office or retail, invested tenants, a broad user base, and buyer and lender demand. Smaller buildings also attract owner-users, adding exit options.

What are the main risks of investing in industrial real estate?

Key risks include tenant default and vacancy, rollover concentration, new speculative supply, functional obsolescence, environmental contamination, rising property taxes, capital costs for roofs and pavement, interest-rate and refinancing risk, and zoning limits on use or outdoor storage. Most are identifiable in diligence. See diligence and risk questions.

How do I start investing in industrial real estate?

Choose public industrial REITs, passive syndications or funds, or direct ownership. Direct buyers often start with a small single-tenant or small-bay building in a familiar market. Assemble a broker, attorney, lender, inspectors and accountant; learn lease and operating statement analysis before offering.

What is an industrial REIT?

An industrial REIT owns and usually operates industrial properties, distributing most taxable income. Public REITs offer liquidity and diversification, but no individual building control; share prices move with the market. Direct ownership offers control and tax-planning options with less liquidity.

What do core, value-add, and opportunistic mean?

They describe risk strategies. Core is stabilized, well-located property with long leases and low leverage. Core-plus adds modest upside. Value-add involves leasing up vacancy, renovating, or re-tenanting to raise income. Opportunistic includes development, heavy repositioning, or distressed situations with the highest risk and expected return. See value-add vs. core.

What is the difference between an owner-user and an investor buyer?

Owner-users buy for occupancy, location, layout and control; investors buy tenant income. Owner-users may pay more for vacant or soon-vacant buildings, especially with SBA 504 financing; investors pay for existing leases. The fitting buyer pool shapes pricing and marketing. See owner-user vs. investor.

What is a 1031 exchange, and how does it work for industrial property?

A Section 1031 exchange can defer federal capital gains tax when proceeds from an investment property sale are reinvested in another qualifying property. IRS rules generally require identification within 45 days and acquisition within 180 days, with proceeds held by a qualified intermediary. Industrial property may be exchanged for other real property held for investment or business use. Consult a tax advisor. See 1031 exchange.

What is a sale-leaseback?

A business sells its occupied building to an investor and simultaneously leases it back long term. Equity becomes working capital without moving operations; the buyer gets a known tenant. Rent and lease terms drive price. See the selling guide’s sale-leaseback section.

How do you finance an industrial property purchase?

Sources include banks, credit unions, life companies, CMBS lenders, debt funds, and SBA 504 or 7(a) loans for owner-users. Lenders test LTV, debt-service coverage, and sometimes debt yield, as well as lease term, tenant credit, and environmental reports. Compare rate, term, amortization, recourse, reserves, and prepayment terms. See financing in the investment guide.

Valuation and underwriting

What is a cap rate?

Cap rate is net operating income divided by price or value. A building with $100,000 NOI selling for $1.5 million has a rate of about 6.7 percent. Lower rates mean higher prices relative to income. Check whether a quote uses in-place, projected, or adjusted income. Try the cap-rate calculator.

What is a good cap rate for industrial property?

No single cap rate is “good.” It depends on rates, location, building quality, lease term, tenant credit, market rent, and capital needs. A low rate with a strong tenant and long lease may be better than a high rate with near-term vacancy. Compare similar sales and current borrowing costs.

How is an industrial property valued?

Appraisers and buyers use three approaches: income (capitalized or discounted NOI), usually best for leased investments; sales comparison, often best for owner-user properties; and cost (land plus depreciated replacement cost) as a check. See how buyers underwrite.

What is net operating income (NOI)?

NOI is rental and other property income minus operating expenses (taxes, insurance, repairs, utilities, and management), before debt service, depreciation, income taxes, and usually capital reserves. Net-lease reimbursements mean NOI is near base rent less unrecovered costs. See NOI.

How useful is price per square foot for industrial property?

Price per square foot helps compare owner-user and vacant buildings but hides differences in land, clear height, office, yard, condition, and lease terms. Use it with income value and replacement cost, comparing similar buildings in the same submarket and size range.

What is WALT, and why does it matter?

WALT is remaining lease term weighted by rent or square footage. Longer WALT supports predictable income and financing; shorter WALT adds rollover risk but may let rents reset to market. See WALT.

What are DSCR and LTV?

DSCR (debt service coverage ratio) divides NOI by annual loan payments; lenders commonly require above 1.0, often 1.25 or higher. LTV (loan-to-value) divides loan amount by property value. The lower loan allowed by these tests usually controls. See DSCR and LTV.

What are IRR, equity multiple, and cash-on-cash return?

Cash-on-cash is annual pre-tax cash flow divided by equity invested. Equity multiple is total cash returned divided by equity over the hold. IRR annualizes returns based on the timing of all cash flows, including sale proceeds. Each measures something different. See IRR.

How do you determine market rent for an industrial building?

Market rent comes from signed leases for comparable space (size, location, clear height, doors, office, and condition), adjusted for free rent and improvement allowances. Asking rents show expectations, not deals. Ask brokers or appraisers for lease comparables.

How do interest rates affect industrial property values?

Higher rates raise borrowing costs and required returns, tending to raise cap rates and lower prices unless rent growth offsets them. Lower rates generally reverse this. Effects are not one-for-one; rent growth, supply and investor demand also matter. Model realistic financing, not the lowest available rate.

Selling an industrial property

How do I sell an industrial building?

Obtain a broker opinion and organize leases and financials. Choose broad or targeted marketing, review offers, select a buyer through an LOI, negotiate the purchase agreement, support diligence and close. The selling guide explains.

How long does it take to sell an industrial property?

A sale typically takes several months for preparation, marketing, offers, negotiation, diligence (often 30 to 60 days), and closing. Environmental findings, financing, estoppels, title, or 1031 timing can extend it. Organize documents before marketing. See the sale process.

How do I find out what my industrial building is worth?

Request a broker opinion using comparable sales, leases, and buyer demand, or commission an appraisal for a lender, estate, or partnership. Provide the rent roll, leases, expenses, tax bills, and building details. See pricing.

What documents do I need to sell an industrial property?

Gather leases and amendments, rent roll, two to three years of operating statements, tax bills and appeal history, service contracts, title policy, survey, environmental and condition reports, permits and occupancy certificates, warranties, utility information, and site plans. See preparing documents.

Is it better to sell an industrial building vacant or leased?

Vacant buildings may attract owner-users willing to pay a premium for the right location and function; leased buildings attract income buyers. Long, market-rate leases often command stronger investor pricing. Short or below-market leases can hurt both buyer pools.

What does an industrial investment sales broker do?

A sales broker prices and markets property, qualifies buyers, manages tours, confidentiality and offers, negotiates, and coordinates diligence and closing. Sellers usually pay a percentage commission at closing under the listing agreement. Choose recent submarket experience.

Should I sell off-market or with a full marketing process?

Broad marketing reaches more buyers and may produce a stronger price. Off-market offers privacy, less tenant disruption, and speed, but can leave money on the table. A short list of qualified buyers is a middle path. See confidentiality and marketing.

What taxes apply when I sell an industrial building?

Federal capital gains tax generally applies to the gain, and depreciation taken is recaptured. For real property, unrecaptured Section 1250 gain is taxed at a federal rate of up to 25 percent. Illinois income tax and, in some cases, the net investment income tax may also apply, and state, county, and local transfer taxes are due at closing. A 1031 exchange can defer gain. Plan with a tax advisor before you list.

What happens after I accept an offer on my industrial property?

The buyer signs the purchase agreement, deposits earnest money and reviews inspections, environmental, title, survey, leases and estoppels. After diligence, the deposit typically becomes non-refundable except under specified conditions; financing and closing follow. See PSA and earnest money.

How do I prepare my industrial building for sale?

Organize documents, resolve deferred maintenance that buyers will price aggressively (roof leaks, dock equipment, pavement, life safety), clean up the yard, confirm permits and zoning compliance, address known environmental questions, and line up tenant renewals where possible. See physical preparation.

Due diligence and environmental

What is due diligence when buying an industrial property?

Before committing to close, buyers review leases, income, condition, environmental history, title, survey, zoning, permits, utilities, taxes and insurance. Findings support proceeding, renegotiating or walking away. See the due diligence guide.

What is a Phase I Environmental Site Assessment?

A Phase I ESA reviews records, inspects the site, and interviews relevant parties to identify recognized environmental conditions. Performed under ASTM E1527-21 to satisfy EPA’s All Appropriate Inquiries rule, it may help buyers qualify for certain CERCLA protections. It does not sample soil or groundwater. See environmental diligence.

How long is a Phase I ESA good for?

All Appropriate Inquiries generally requires key Phase I components completed or updated within 180 days before acquisition; reports over one year old generally need a new assessment. Lenders may impose their own requirements. Match timing to closing.

What is a Phase II environmental assessment?

A Phase II samples soil, groundwater, vapor, or building materials to test suspected contamination flagged in a Phase I. It is common at sites with historic manufacturing, tanks, dry cleaning, or chemical use. Results may lead to remediation, price changes, insurance, or withdrawal.

What are common environmental issues at industrial sites?

Frequent issues include leaking underground or aboveground storage tanks, chlorinated solvents from degreasing, petroleum, metals, PCBs in older equipment, asbestos and lead in building materials, vapor intrusion, fill material of unknown origin, and emerging contaminants such as PFAS. Adjacent properties can also contaminate a site through groundwater.

What is a property condition assessment?

An engineer reviews roof, structure, paving, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, fire protection and accessibility, estimating repair costs and a replacement schedule. Industrial priorities include roof age, docks, slab, sprinklers and truck-court pavement. See physical diligence.

What is an ALTA survey, and why do lenders want one?

An ALTA/NSPS survey maps boundaries, easements, encroachments, improvements, access, and flood zone to national standards, helping a title insurer remove the standard survey exception. Lenders typically require one. It can confirm truck access, parking, rail spurs, and property-line conflicts. See ALTA survey.

What does a title commitment show, and what does title insurance cover?

A title commitment lists the owner, legal description, liens, easements, restrictions, and closing requirements. Title insurance covers certain non-excluded defects, such as forged documents or undisclosed liens. Review each exception; easements and restrictions can limit site use. See title commitment.

What is a tenant estoppel certificate?

A tenant signs an estoppel confirming rent, term, deposits, options, amendments and either party's defaults. Buyers and lenders use it to verify the seller's lease representations. See estoppel certificate.

What is an Illinois No Further Remediation (NFR) letter?

Illinois EPA typically issues NFR letters through its Site Remediation or leaking UST program after cleanup objectives are met for a defined use. Land-use restrictions or engineered barriers often require maintenance and recording. Read conditions; “clean” does not mean suitable for every use.

Illinois zoning and property taxes

What zoning does a warehouse or industrial use need?

Industrial uses generally need manufacturing or industrial zoning. Municipalities define permitted, special and prohibited uses; outdoor storage, truck terminals and heavy manufacturing are often restricted. County zoning applies in unincorporated areas. Obtain the designation and written use confirmation from the local authority.

What is a Planned Manufacturing District (PMD) in Chicago?

Chicago PMD zoning protects industrial business concentrations from residential and other non-industrial conversion by restricting incompatible uses. Check the City zoning map for boundaries and permissions. See the Chicago research guide.

What are Chicago’s industrial corridors?

City-designated industrial concentrations guide Chicago land-use policy and economic-development programs. Some corridors have been reviewed or modernized for broader mixed uses. Confirm current designations and zoning with City planning and zoning departments.

What is the difference between a special use and a variance?

A special or conditional use is allowed after public review, often with conditions. A variance grants relief from a standard such as setback, parking, or height when strict application creates hardship. Both require applications and hearings, so allow time. See special use and variance.

What is different about industrial property in unincorporated areas?

County rules govern zoning, permits, and often utilities and police outside municipal boundaries. Rules and costs may differ from a neighboring village, and annexation can change them. Confirm jurisdiction and utilities. See unincorporated vs. incorporated.

How are industrial property taxes calculated in Illinois?

The assessor estimates market value and applies an assessment level; a state or county equalization factor may follow. After exemptions, multiply assessed value by the combined rates of local taxing districts, whose levies set the rates. See the property tax guide.

Why are Cook County industrial property taxes different from the collar counties?

Cook County assesses industrial property at 25 percent of market value, reassesses by region every three years, and applies a state equalizer. Other counties generally use one-third of market value, township assessors, and annual equalization. District tax rates also differ. See Cook vs. collar counties.

What is the Cook County Class 6b incentive?

Class 6b lowers assessments for qualifying new, substantially rehabilitated, or reoccupied industrial property. The level is commonly 10 percent of market value for ten years, then 15 and 20 percent in the final two years, versus 25 percent standard. Municipal support and timely application are required. Confirm current rules with the Cook County Assessor. See Cook County incentives.

How do I appeal industrial property taxes in Illinois?

In Cook County, appeal to the Assessor during the township window, then the Board of Review; elsewhere, appeal to the county Board of Review. Further appeals may go to the state Property Tax Appeal Board or circuit court. Deadlines are short; income, comparable sales, vacancy, or appraisal evidence matters. See appeals and deadlines.

Who pays real estate transfer tax in Illinois?

Illinois and county transfer taxes usually fall on sellers by custom. Some municipalities add taxes under local ordinances; Chicago's falls largely on buyers. Confirm rates, exemptions and responsibility with your attorney and municipality. See transfer tax.

The Chicago industrial market

Why is Chicago a major industrial and logistics hub?

Chicago connects all six Class I railroads and major interstates I-55, I-57, I-80, I-88, I-90, and I-94. O’Hare is a major air-cargo airport, and the region’s workforce and central location put much of the U.S. within one or two days by truck. See the logistics corridors guide.

How big is the Chicago industrial market?

Chicagoland is among North America’s largest industrial markets, with more than a billion square feet in most brokerage counts. Definitions of area, building size, and property type vary, so compare like-for-like reports.

What are the main industrial submarkets in Chicagoland?

Reports commonly divide the region into O’Hare, Chicago, I-55, I-80/Joliet, I-88, North and Northwest suburbs and Lake County, South and Southwest suburbs, Fox Valley, southeast Wisconsin, and northwest Indiana. Boundaries vary by firm. See Chicagoland submarkets and corridors.

What are the I-55 and I-80 industrial corridors?

I-55 runs southwest through Bolingbrook and Romeoville toward Joliet; I-80 crosses Will County around Joliet, New Lenox, and Minooka. Nearby intermodal terminals anchor much of the region’s modern distribution space. See the freight corridor table.

Why is the O’Hare area important for industrial real estate?

Elk Grove Village, Franklin Park, Bensenville, Wood Dale, and Des Plaines combine O’Hare cargo, interstate and rail access, and older small- and mid-size buildings near the city. Limited land makes it an infill market where functional locations tend to stay leased.

What intermodal terminals serve Chicagoland?

Class I railroads operate intermodal terminals across Chicagoland. Will County’s BNSF Logistics Park Chicago in Elwood and Union Pacific’s Joliet terminal anchor the I-55/I-80 market, alongside CenterPoint Intermodal Center. See the corridors guide.

What is the industrial vacancy rate in Chicago?

Chicago’s industrial vacancy changes quarterly by submarket, size, and age; large distribution and small infill buildings can trend differently. Compare current brokerage reports using consistent definitions. The home page links recent news.

Are Chicago industrial rents going up?

Chicago rents rose with demand over recent years, but growth varies by submarket and new supply. Small-bay and infill rents may differ from big-box rents. Use recent signed comparables for your submarket and size range, not regional averages.

Which Chicagoland county is best for industrial property?

It depends on use. Cook offers infill access, labor, and O’Hare, but higher taxes; Will has intermodal access and modern buildings; DuPage and Lake have established parks; Kane, McHenry, Kendall, DeKalb, and Grundy offer more land and growth corridors. The nine-county guide links assessor and planning contacts.

How do I find industrial property for sale in Chicagoland?

Check commercial listings; many small and mid-size buildings trade through brokers or off-market. Set size, county, clear height, door, yard and lease criteria with an industrial investment sales broker.
