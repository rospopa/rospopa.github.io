Truck dimensions and turning paths

Published and sources checked October 6, 2026. Metric conversions are rounded.

By Pavlo Rospopa · Last updated October 10, 2026

In brief

Use these sourced examples to assess loading, gates, driveways, yards, and truck courts. Cargo capacity, legal road dimensions, and maneuvering space are different measurements.

  • A 53-foot trailer is not a 53-foot tractor-trailer combination. A box-truck body size is not the overall truck length.
  • A container's dimensions alone do not describe the tractor, chassis, loaded height, or turning envelope.
  • There is no universal truck turning radius or safe truck-court depth. Require fleet-specific swept-path review by a qualified engineer.
  • Owner lens: A site constraint can narrow future users even when the building itself is functional. Confirm recorded access and easements, gates, staging, and fleet-specific swept paths before valuing yard or loading improvements.

Tractors and semitrailers

A tractor alone includes cab and power unit; wheelbase, axles, sleeper and fifth-wheel position vary. A tractor plus semitrailer is articulated; overall length depends on both vehicles and their connection. Do not guess tractor length or treat a trailer label as rig length.

Semitrailer length references, not overall combination dimensions
ReferenceImperialMetricWhat the number means
48-foot semitrailer48 ft14.63 mFHWA describes the minimum semitrailer length states must allow on the National Network and reasonable access routes, subject to grandfathered provisions. Not interior cargo length.
53-foot semitrailer53 ft16.15 mFHWA's Illinois grandfathered-length table lists 53 ft. This is the trailer length reference, not an overall rig length or a turning template.

FHWA states there is no federal overall-length limit for tractor-semitrailer combinations on the National Network and reasonable access routes. Its general commercial-vehicle width limit is 102 in (8 ft 6 in; about 2.6 m), subject to exceptions. Neither statement confirms local-road or driveway access. Check current Illinois rules, route restrictions, permits, axle positions, and fleet dimensions.

There is no federal commercial-vehicle height requirement. FHWA describes most state limits as 13 ft 6 in to 14 ft (about 4.11 to 4.27 m), subject to lower clearances and exceptions. This does not authorize a height in Illinois; check the route and current state and local rules.

Straight and box trucks

A straight truck’s cargo body shares the cab’s chassis. Rental examples below give interior cargo-box dimensions, not exterior bodies, door openings, overall lengths or roof heights.

Penske says dimensions vary by manufacturer. Neither cited rental page publishes overall external vehicle length. Its commercial light-duty range lists 12- and 16-foot box trucks and 16- and 18-foot cabovers; these labels are not universal specifications.

For a 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 22, 24, or 26 ft body (about 3.66, 4.27, 4.88, 5.49, 6.10, 6.71, 7.32, or 7.92 m), obtain a model-specific body/chassis drawing. These conversions do not establish availability or interior dimensions. Separately verify overall length, exterior width including protrusions, loaded and unloaded height, wheelbase, rear overhang, door opening, and liftgate deployment.

Containers and chassis

20- and 40-foot dry containers, including high cubes, are containers alone, not road vehicles. Nominal lengths are not usable interior dimensions. A high cube has a taller body; chassis deck, tires, suspension, and load add height in road transport.

Source: Maersk dry equipment specifications (PDF, page 1). These rounded size classes are separate from interior and door-opening dimensions. Metric values convert the nominal sizes; they are not precise ISO dimensions or measurements of a specific unit. They exclude chassis and tractor. Obtain equipment drawings for clearance work.

Request the carrier's drawing for the exact equipment's exterior, interior, and door opening. For a chassis combination, also obtain chassis length, axle positions, container placement, loaded height, and tractor geometry. The container label cannot establish overall height or turning clearance. ISO 668 is the dimensional/classification standard; its full specification is not reproduced here.

Turning circle, swept path, and offtracking

Turning circle diameter vs. radius
Diameter spans a circle; radius is half only when both use the same circle and measurement convention. A curb-to-curb wheel path is not a wall-to-wall body envelope. Ask which reference point, steering lock, speed, and maneuver were measured.
Swept path and outer sweep
A swept path is the area a vehicle occupies during a maneuver. Its outer boundary includes front corners, mirrors, body overhang, and trailer swing, not just tire tracks.
Offtracking
Rear wheels and a trailer do not follow the tractor's front-wheel path in a low-speed turn; they commonly track inward. Trailer length, wheelbase, axle locations, articulation, steering, and approach angle change the path.
Design vehicle
AASHTO labels such as WB-62 and WB-67 describe specific geometric templates, not interchangeable fleet measurements or a universal radius. A qualified engineer must select a current design vehicle and compare it with the site's fleet and maneuvers.

Cited fleet specifications do not establish a complete configuration’s turning radius, so none is given. Forward circulation, dock backing, opposing traffic and queuing require different checks. FHWA road-size limits are not turning templates.

Site-review checklist

  1. Identify the fleet: exact tractor, trailer, straight-truck, or container/chassis configuration, wheelbases, axle positions, body projections, height, and operating loads.
  2. Survey the constraints: gate openings, curb returns, driveways, dock angles, court depth, grade changes, fences, bollards, poles, parked vehicles, easements, fire access, snow storage, and overhead obstructions.
  3. Test the maneuver: have a qualified civil/transportation engineer perform fleet-specific swept-path analysis for entry, turns, backing, unloading, and exit, including required operational margins and conflicts.
  4. Check access and operation: local truck routes, bridge/underpass restrictions, delivery rules, pavement capacity, loading equipment, and municipal approvals. Legal road width or nominal cargo size is not a guaranteed safe clearance.

Also use the building evaluation checklist, building specifications and industrial due-diligence guide.

Primary sources and limitations

  • FHWA: Federal Size Regulations for Commercial Motor Vehicles, including the Illinois semitrailer grandfathered-length table. This explanatory report is not a current local-route approval; actual regulations govern.
  • Penske 16-foot truck and Penske 26-foot truck: approximate interior dimensions that vary by manufacturer.
  • Penske commercial light-duty trucks: published fleet labels, not overall vehicle drawings.
  • ISO 668 catalog: container classification/dimension standard; full tables require access to the standard.
  • Maersk dry equipment specifications (PDF): published nominal outside-size classes for standard and high-cube steel containers, with separate interior and door-opening specifications.
  • AASHTO publications: the Green Book contains design-vehicle guidance. Its paid dimensional/turning templates are not reproduced or replaced with guessed values.
Scope. Educational reference, not engineering, construction, traffic, safety, or legal advice. No table here authorizes a clearance, maneuver, vehicle movement, or site design. Confirm specifications and current requirements with the fleet, carrier, responsible authorities, and qualified engineers.
